Note: This is a rant, a point on the horizon I want to aim towards, but is under-specified. I'm going to be expanding on what I mean by this in the future.

The new computer science curriculum needs to abandon teaching a single language. It's important to understand the *shape* of computation in a variety of forms instead of simply learning the details of *a* programming language.

This is not an argument for "breadth vs. depth", it is an argument for depth by other means: learning to translate between different programming languages as part of homework, being shown examples of a fictional language and trying to infer the rules, writing comparisons about how different concepts and ideas are expressed by different languages and understanding the comparative advantage in different domains.

The new curriculum needs to teach the *algebra* of programming, how to sketch out even reasonably complex code's behavior on pen and paper and understand when steps in a computation can be commuted or separated or when two programs that look very different are, in fact, the same computation.

The new curriculum includes *more* math and logic, it include introduction to proof assistants and formal methods. After their first year, a student should be able to describe the Curry-Howard correspondence and its implications and be able to read and write simple proofs in dependently typed theory.

The next generation of students needs to be introduced to reading research papers, especially formative papers in the history of computer science. The future CS student needs to be grounded enough in all the work that has come before that they can *guide* the machine with their superior research vision rather than being puppeted by an agent in a loop of nearly automatic approvals.

These students must be able to write code as a way of clarifying their thoughts and to read clever, even obtuse, code made by agents. They must to be able to specify theorems, properties, tests that code must satisfy.

They must be able to work at any level of abstraction, high or low, and be able to articulate the architecture of a large project.

These students must be excellent at communicating, not just with the machine but with other people. They need to be good collaborators, good delegators, good managers, even at the beginning of their careers.

In other words, future computer science students will need to learn even more, even faster, than we did. Yet, if they can they'll be able to reach *incredible* heights.

The question isn't whether computer science will continue, it will, but rather whether we will adapt to give students the education and experience they need or whether they will have to carve this out for themselves.
